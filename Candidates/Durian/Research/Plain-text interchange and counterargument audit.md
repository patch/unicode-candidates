## Finding

This bounded audit found no public evidence of a present demand, workaround or concrete loss concerning interchange of an artwork-independent durian symbol in plain text. The reviewed workflows currently communicate their rules through ordinary wording and documents, while a complete sign or image asset carries the pictogram’s visual display. Because no standardized durian character exists, however, the absence of direct character interchange is expected. It does not show that relevant users would reject, ignore or derive no value from such a character if it were encoded.

The recurring pictogram has a communicative function. It can make a displayed restriction compact, salient and less dependent on one language, and five functional designs in the [contributor corpus](<Contributor photo corpus synthesis.md>) support one generic fruit referent rather than dependence on one artwork. A future character could supply that artwork-independent referent in plain text while words, prohibition marks, hands or layout supply the larger rule. The reviewed public sources do not establish whether users would adopt the character for that purpose, which operations they would perform with it or what present limitation they experience without it.

## Question and boundary

The audit asks whether the documented generic durian pictogram, separated from prohibition circles, slashes, hands, wording, layout, branding and exact artwork, is needed as a public plain-text interchange unit. It tests the strongest encoding and non-encoding explanations in three recurring workflow classes:

1. transit and carriage prohibitions;
2. accommodation and building rules; and
3. retail handling instructions.

The evidence corpus comprises the existing six-territory signage record, the public-safe contributor synthesis and a bounded review of current public sources. The review stopped at 18 materially distinct external sources plus the contributor synthesis: the approved ceiling was 25, each workflow class had at least one direct source, and further searches repeated the same document and sign patterns without exposing a documented demand, workaround or concrete loss.

This pass did not contact people, inspect private originals or private mappings, buy access, broadly collect images, run an unlabelled recognition test, complete the separate character-equivalence or sequence audits, or collect Emoji Frequency evidence. It does not decide Emoji selection factors.

## Method

For each workflow, the audit sought four things:

- a named or reasonably identifiable sender and recipient;
- the systems or artefacts through which the information moves;
- any current workaround or proposed operation on the prospective unit, rather than on the whole sign or rule; and
- a concrete limitation under ordinary words, existing characters or sequences, an image or icon asset, metadata, markup or a higher-level system.

The current Script Encoding Working Group guidance requires a case for need for public interchange in plain text [U07](Sources.md#u07). An affirmative Character-route result does not require an already encoded character or direct Unicode interchange. It could instead arise from a named need to preserve the artwork-independent unit, an explicit prospective use, a surrogate such as a private code or image placeholder, or a concrete operation made materially harder by existing representations. Conversely, current use of words or images does not by itself support non-encoding: those are the available representations today. An adverse result would require evidence that relevant users or systems do not need the prospective semantic unit, would prefer existing representations for the same operation or would gain no material function from encoding. This audit did not obtain that evidence.

Searches combined the terms ‘durian’, ‘no durian’, ‘no durians’, ‘prohibited’, ‘house rules’, ‘baggage’, ‘do not press’, ‘avoid pressing’, ‘sign’, ‘pictogram’, ‘icon’, ‘asset’ and ‘metadata’, with limited Thai checks using ‘ทุเรียน’, ‘ห้าม’ and transit terms. Source types included official operator and venue rules, legal or policy pages, one existing scholarly hotel-sign corpus, public image metadata and official standards pages. The multilingual boundary is a limitation: this was not a systematic search in Bahasa Indonesia, Bahasa Melayu, Khmer or Vietnamese.

## Alternative-representation tests

### Ordinary text

Ordinary wording currently expresses the propositions demonstrated in the audited workflows: the named fruit, a broader offensive-smell category, the prohibited place, the handling action and any consequence. It can be multilingual, attached to a room, copied electronically, published on the web, included in booking terms or placed in a PDF. It remains language-specific and does not supply the pictogram’s artwork-independent visual-semantic unit. Its present availability does not show whether users would find an encoded durian character useful alongside it.

### Image or icon assets

An image or icon file preserves exact artwork, including whole or cut fruit, colour, prohibition marks, hands and layout. It is therefore the appropriate representation when that exact visual display is the object of exchange. A character would serve a different purpose: an artwork-independent semantic unit whose glyph may vary. The two representations can be complementary, and the sources do not establish whether users need that additional textual layer.

### Metadata, markup and accessibility text

Web Content Accessibility Guidelines 2.2 requires text alternatives for non-text content, and W3C technique G94 explains that an alternative should preserve the same purpose and information [I08](Sources.md#i08) [I09](Sources.md#i09). These sources establish a general representation capability, not operator-specific implementation for the audited durian signs. In an accessible digital record, a caption or alternative such as ‘No durian’ can carry the rule while the graphic remains an image. Public image archives currently identify sign files through ordinary-language titles, descriptions and categories [S01](Sources.md#s01). Text alternatives and metadata could coexist with an encoded character; their availability does not establish that the character would be redundant.

### Managed graphical-symbol systems

ISO 7001 describes public-information symbols as graphical originals that can be reproduced at different sizes and used with text [I10](Sources.md#i10). ISO 7000 demonstrates a related higher-level model in which equipment symbols have reference numbers, titles, representations, functions and intended-use metadata [I11](Sources.md#i11). ISO 9186-3 separately provides referent-association testing and recognizes supplementary text where the symbol alone is insufficient [I12](Sources.md#i12). These standards do not register a durian symbol or prove that any reviewed operator uses such a system. They show one possible management model for graphical symbols, not that this model satisfies every prospective plain-text operation or would be preferred to an encoded character.

### Existing characters and sequences

The prior bounded Unicode 17.0 review found no character or standardized sequence representing durian. That removes one possible semantic equivalent but does not itself establish interchange need. Words and phrases express the current rules, but the sources do not determine whether they are adequate substitutes for every prospective use of an artwork-independent durian symbol. The complete candidate-specific equivalence and sequence matrices remain separate work and were not expanded into this audit.

## Counterarguments tested

| Proposition favouring encoding | Finding in this audit |
| --- | --- |
| Repeated independent signage implies a textual symbol | It supports a recurring pictographic convention and a generic referent. Repetition alone does not establish plain-text need, but it supplies a plausible unit whose prospective textual use must be investigated |
| A language-light pictogram preserves something words cannot | It can reduce language dependence and improve salience. Current multilingual wording does not prove that the pictographic unit would add no value if encoded |
| A character would make the symbol searchable and archivable | This is a plausible prospective capability. Current archives use filenames, captions, categories, document text and image assets because no standardized character exists; their present practice neither proves nor disproves future use |
| Variation across designs shows an abstract unit | Variation strengthens independence from one artwork, but it is also consistent with different illustrations of the same real-world object. User conception and prospective artwork-independent operation remain unproved |
| A private icon code or asset identifier might reveal an interoperability problem | No public operator asset catalogue, icon-font mapping, private-use mapping or cross-organization conversion failure was found. This leaves the favourable possibility unsubstantiated rather than rebutted |

## Negative results, access limits and falsification

The search found no public Durian-specific sign standard, asset catalogue, icon identifier, font mapping, content-management field, interchange format, accessibility implementation, designer specification or cross-organization conversion report. Repeated searches increasingly returned photographs, hotel rules, travel commentary and copies of the same prohibition wording. No additional workflow class appeared, so the audit stopped below its source ceiling. This is a failure to locate affirmative need evidence within the boundary, not evidence that prospective use would not arise after encoding.

The Singapore regulations page supplied current legal context, but the accessible record did not establish an express durian clause; it must not be cited as one. The Royal Brunei page exposed its relevant text through current indexing while the rendered page was not reliably extractable. The public FairPrice report is supplementary: its event year is not disclosed in the rendered page, its account is anonymous and the page records no retailer response. No inference about a corporate sign standard or design lineage rests on it.

Any of the following would supply the missing affirmative evidence:

- independent users assigning the same artwork-independent identifier to the generic durian unit;
- public exchange between systems that cannot preserve the unit through words, images, metadata or a documented higher-level protocol;
- a recurring need to input, quote, search, index, parse, collate, provide fallback for or archive that unit as plain text; or
- incompatible private mappings that produce an actual interoperability failure rather than a merely hypothetical inconvenience.

## Decision and project consequence

This pass did not establish the affirmative Character-route need, because it found no documented present demand, workaround or concrete loss. It also did not support non-encoding: direct character interchange cannot be expected before the character exists, and the current use of words, images or metadata does not establish whether relevant users would adopt or benefit from an encoded semantic unit. A Character proposal should remain deferred because the need evidence is missing, not because the audit found future use unlikely. The result does not settle abstract-character identity or decide the Emoji route.

The public-source phase of the Character interchange task is complete. The smallest decision-bearing Character follow-up now requires project-owner authority for human consultation with operators, wayfinding or sign-system designers and text-system users. An interview or written questionnaire should ask:

1. Is the durian graphic maintained as a named reusable symbol separate from complete sign artwork, and what identifier is used?
2. Who commissions, edits, approves and reuses it, and is the same semantic unit shared across organizations?
3. Which sign schedules, asset libraries, content-management systems, databases, CAD or building-information systems store it?
4. How is it represented today when exported, copied as plain text or moved between systems: wording, filename, image reference, metadata, private code or something else?
5. If a standardized character existed, would you use it for input, composition, quotation, search, indexing, parsing, accessibility, translation, audit or archival retrieval, and why?
6. What concrete limitation exists today, and what would or would not improve if the character were available alongside words, images, metadata or a private identifier?
7. Must whole, paired and cut-open forms be interchangeable, and when does exact rendering matter?
8. Do incompatible mappings already cause a public interoperability failure?

Until that authority and evidence are available, the highest-priority actionable central task is the separate bounded Emoji factor and query-plan pass. The later unlabelled recognition test remains separate and can inform both routes.
